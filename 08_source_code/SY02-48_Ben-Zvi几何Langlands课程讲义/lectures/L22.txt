Lecture 22.
Spectral decomposition in topological ﬁeld theory: 4/15/21
Recall that we began discussing local operators, which we think of as measuring the ﬁelds at (or near) a
point. The main structure on local operators is operator product expansion, which is a multiplication. Given
two points x, y that are nearby, we evaluate the local operator in topological ﬁeld theory by excising small
spheres around these two points and getting a bordism from two spheres to the empty set. But we could also
take a larger sphere around both x and y, giving us a single local operator that we can evaluate in the same
way: we get a bordism Sn−1 →∅, and our local operator deﬁnes a state in Z(Sn−1).
If our theory were not conformal, this is a little trickier: to deﬁne a single local operator, we shrink the
sphere around the point, taking the limit as the radius goes to zero. To perform operator product expansion,
we must also bring x and y closer to each other.
Back to the topological case — operator product expansion takes place entirely within the ball B bounded
by the larger sphere. Remove the two smaller balls around x and y inside B; then B minus these spheres
deﬁnes a bordism
(22.1a)
Sn−1 ⨿Sn−1 −→Sn−1,
which the TFT sends to
(22.1b)
∗: Z(Sn−1) ⊗Z(Sn−1) −→Z(Sn−1).
This is the operator product expansion map, and hopefully it looks like a multiplication. More precisely, for
any pair of two spheres inside a larger sphere, we obtain such a map, and the general structure is a family of
maps on the conﬁguration space of two small (n −1)-spheres inside a larger space. Because this theory is
topological, this family must be locally constant.
Example 22.2. When n = 1, we are looking at pairs of intervals inside a larger line. This conﬁguration
space is disconnected: π0 knows whether x is to the left of y or not. Therefore operator product expansion is
not in general commutative for 1-dimensional TFT: Oy ∗O′
x̸ = Ox ∗O′
y.
However, it is associative: the conﬁguration space of three intervals in a larger interval with a speciﬁed
order is connected.

When n > 1, the conﬁguration space of pairs of discs in a larger disc is connected, and therefore Z(Sn−1)
is an associative, commutative algebra. There is enough room to move operators around — this is subtle in
non-topological QFTs, but is particularly nice in the topological case. And in either case, this commutativity
is completely absent in quantum mechanics (n = 1, for which OPE is only associative).
Remark 22.3. Next time, we will work in the derived setting, and then this story gets more complicated.
We will see more information about the conﬁguration space in the derived notion of commutativity that we
obtain on Z(Sn−1).

Given a commutative algebra, we want to spectrally decompose it, so let Maﬀ:= Spec(Z(Sn−1)), which is
called the (TODO: I think) moduli of vacua of the theory. Given a local operator O and a “vacuum” |0⟩
(meaning a point on Maﬀ), we can deﬁne a vacuum expectation value (VEV) of O, denoted ⟨0 | O | 0⟩: an
operator is a function on Maﬀ. In TFT, we will use this as a deﬁnition of a vacuum.
The slogan is that a topological ﬁeld theory (or, its local operators) spectrally decomposes over Maﬀ:
everything you can calculate in a TFT is linear over Z(Sn−1), so decomposes over its spectrum.

And we mean everything. For example, let M be an (n −1)-manifold. We will construct a Z(Sn−1)-action
on Z(M). Speciﬁcally, consider the cylinder M × [0, 1] and excise a small ball around t = 1/2 and some point
in M. This modiﬁed manifold is a bordism M ⨿Sn−1 →M, hence deﬁnes a map Z(Sn−1) ⊗Z(M) →Z(M)
which is our action map. It depends on the choice of point we chose, but again it’s locally constant, which is
good enough for us (again, things are more nuanced in the derived setting). And another bordism shows
this is compatible with OPE, so we obtain a module structure. Passing to the spectrum, Z(M) deﬁnes a
quasicoherent sheaf on Maﬀ.

In some sense, the Bernstein center Z(C) is universal for this structure. C forms a sheaf of categories over
M := Spec(Z(C)).27 If R is a commutative ring, an R-linear structure on C is equivalent to data of a ring
homomorphism R →Z(C): R acts on the Hom-spaces in compatible ways. Thus the universal such R is
Z(C).
If you’re in a big enough setting (C has enough colimits), R-linearity is equivalent to the idea that C is a
module category for ModR. We’re now doing spectral decomposition of categories, albeit only over aﬃne
spaces.
There are three diﬀerent ways to deﬁne the idea of a sheaf of categories over Spec R.
(1) Sheaﬁfying C over R is data of a map R →Z(C).
(2) Sheaﬁfying C over R means C is enriched in R-modules (equivalently, in quasicoherent sheaves on
Spec R).
(3) Sheaﬁﬁying C over R means an action of the symmetric monoidal category (ModR, ⊗R) on C.

For the last perspective, we know that if M is a free R-module and C ∈C, M ⊗C ∼= C⊕rank(M). In general,
we take a free resolution of M, and therefore obtain a sequence of objects in C. If C has enough colimits, we
can take the colimit of this sequence and obtain something that we call M ⊗C. This action is a categorical
analogue of linearity.
We can also localize: if S ⊂R is a multiplicative subset, we want to be able to localize C over Spec R[S−1],
much like we can localize a quasicoherent sheaf over Spec R[S−1]. We deﬁne the sheaf
(22.8)
C(Spec R[S−1]) = C ⊗ModR ModR[S−1].
Explicitly, this takes C and inverts all morphisms coming from S ⊂R. We will not worry right now about
the precise deﬁnition of the tensor product of categories. But it may help to see that the Hom-sets in C are
already sheaves on R, and localizing C reduces to localizing sheaves/modules for Hom-sets.
Now say A is an associative algebra, such as C[G]. Then ModA sheaﬁﬁes over Spec Z(A), and in particular
RepG sheaﬁﬁes over bG. Therefore, in fact, RepG ≃QC( bG) — but this is too cool to be really true! This is a
noncanonical equivalence.
In fact, you should already be suspicious: it’s tautologically true that RepG sheaﬁﬁes over bG. For S a
subset of bG, the restriction of the sheaf RepG over S is the summand whose characters all lie in S. Letting S
be a point we see what the problem is: to get an identiﬁcation RepG ≃QC( bG), we need to pick isomorphism
classes of G-representations.

Lest you think this is pointless category-wrangling, the analogous statement for representations over R
is false! RepG(R) is a valid category, and bG is deﬁned over R, so it seems like we can be optimistic, but
there are three kinds of reality conditions for irreducible real-valued representations V : Schur’s lemma tells
us End(V ) is a division algebra over a ﬁeld extension of R, so our options are R, C, and H. The complex
representations (End(V ) ∼= C) correspond to C-points of bG which are not R-points, or said diﬀerently that
characters are not real-valued. And even for the real points, we don’t always get End(V ) ∼= R: if End(V ) ∼= H,
we explicitly obtain something not isomorphic to QC( bG). The structure we have is that RepG(R) is an
invertible QC( bG)-module category, but over a ﬁeld that isn’t algebraically closed, this module is not always
trivial. It is in a sense a “categorical line bundle” for QC( bG), which is known by the shorter name of a gerbe.
Said diﬀerently, RepG(R) is an element of the Brauer group of QC( bG), much like H is a nonzero element of
the Brauer group of R.
This is closely related to anomalies in physics. C sheaﬁﬁes over M, but that doesn’t mean it’s the simplest
thing which sheaﬁﬁes over M (which is QC(M)). It could be a gerbe, or even something more complicated.
Any random associative algebra might not even have a nonzero center, in which case what we get is very far
from QC(Z(A)).
