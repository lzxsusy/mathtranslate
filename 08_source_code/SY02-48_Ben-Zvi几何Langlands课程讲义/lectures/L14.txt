Lecture 14.
Tannakian reconstruction: 3/11/21
The stacks lecture will be recorded and posted in the near future, to be watched asynchronously if you
want to learn a crash course in stacks or refresh the material for its use in this course.
Today is about Tannakian reconstruction. The slogan here is that “commutativity indicates geometry:” a
commutative ring is an algebra of functions on a geometric object; we will categorify this. Speciﬁcally, if R is
a commutative ring, ModR has a tensor product M, N 7→M ⊗R N, which is also an R-module. This gives
ModR the structure of a symmetric monoidal category.
The deﬁnition of a symmetric monoidal category is a bit technical. What we care about (which is not
equivalent to the standard deﬁnition) is abelian categories with a few niceness properties, including arbitrary
direct sums. Grothendieck abelian categories satisfy these operations. Such a category C has a symmetric
monoidal structure when we deﬁne a functor ⊗: C × C →C caled the tensor product and an object 1 ∈C
called the unit, and some further data and conditions. These include encoding the fact that tensoring with
the unit is trivial, which is implemented as natural transformations
(14.1)
1 ⊗– ≃– ⊗1 ≃id,
as well as natural transformations encoding associativity and commutativity of ⊗(up to natural isomorphism),
and some conditions on that data. There is a corresponding notion of symmetric monoidal functor which
preserves the data of the tensor product and unit.
The point is, for R a commutative ring, ModR is a symmetric monoidal category with ⊗R and unit R.
Before, we just used R-modules for spectral decomposition, but now we will think of ModR as a categoriﬁed
commutative ring. This will be helpful in TFT, where as the codimension increases, so too does the category
number.
The geometric space is still X = Spec R, but we will categorify the “ring of functions” on X, replacing R
with ModR = QC(X). In algebraic geometry, most schemes aren’t aﬃne, which is somewhat annoying, and is
a major diﬀerence from the manifolds case. One advantage of categorifying is that many more things are
“aﬃne.” Speciﬁcally, suppose X is a general scheme, which admits an aﬃne open cover U = {Spec(Ri)}; then
the category of quasicoherent sheaves on X is
(14.2)
QC(X) = lim

which can be a deﬁnition or a theorem depending on your approach to QC. Quasicoherent sheaves are
symmetric monoidal with tensor product ⊗OX and unit OX. Moreover, if f : X →Y is a map of schemes,
there is a pullback functor f ∗: QC(Y ) →QC(X) and it is symmetric monoidal: OY pulls back to OX, and
tensor products pull back to tensor products. In fact, this is universal: QC(X) satisﬁes the universal property
that whenever we have a map f : Spec(R) →X, there is a functor f ∗: QC(X) →ModR.
To summarize, just as we had O: Schop →CommRing and a nice correspondence, we have QC: Schop →
⊗-Cat. Moreover, one can generalize to quite general kinds of stacks, deﬁning QC(X) for stacks X. We’ll say
what kinds of stacks we allow in a moment — Artin stacks are good examples, and you can in general think
about quotients of schemes by group actions.

This gives us a great source of symmetric monoidal categories: there are other ways to obtain them, but
this is our home base, the categories that feel most like geometry and are most our friends. So let’s say,

But what’s really great about this categoriﬁed Spec is that we can understand many non-aﬃne things,
even stacks. These geometric objects do not have enough functions, but they do have enough quasicoherent
sheaves. Schemes have lots of sheaves, such as skyscrapers, even though we don’t have the decategoriﬁed
analogue, δ-functions.
Theorem 14.4 (Tannakian reconstruction). Let X be a geometric stack. Then the “Tannakization” map
X →Spec QC(X) is an isomorphism.
For references, see Lurie [Lur04] and Bhatt and Halpern-Leistner [BHL17]. Anyways, the upshot is that
geometric stacks are “categorically aﬃne,” and the power of the theorem is that just about anything you
might reasonably encounter is a geometric stack. For example, this includes all quascompact schemes. The
full deﬁnition of a geometric stack is a quasicompact stack with aﬃne diagonal, which means something quite
concrete in practice: if X is such a stack, we can build it by taking an aﬃne variety U and an aﬃne groupoid
G acting on U, and X ∼= U//G.
Remark 14.5. The fact that we have “categoriﬁed δ-functions” is not all that surprising: sheaves seem to
categorify both functions and distributions/measures. This is related to the fact that they can both pull back
and push forward.

We will spend the rest of the lecture on examples.
Example 14.6. For our ﬁrst example, suppose X is a quasicompact scheme. We can detect maps Spec R →X
by looking for pullback functors f ∗: QC(X) →ModR, and Theorem 14.4 tells us that these are equivalent. For
example, k-points of X, i.e. maps Spec k →X, are equivalent to pullbacks f ∗: QC(X) →QC(Spec k) = Vectk.
That is, a point is a tensor functor to Vect.
(
The quintessential example is in representation theory. Let G be a group; then RepG is a symmetric
monoidal category (where we consider representations over a chosen ﬁeld k). The unit is the trivial one-
dimensional representation, and the tensor product is the tensor product of vector spaces with diagonal
G-action. This is a symmetric monoidal category naturally in G.
Now we plug this into the Tannakian machine: is Spec(RepG) the category of quasicoherent sheaves on
something? To think about this, we need a little more data. Representations are a vector space plus a
G-action, meaning we can forget the action, deﬁning a faithful symmetric monoidal functor Rep(G) →Vect.
In general, a faithful symmetric monoidal functor to Vect is called a ﬁber functor. This is special: we never
have this for QC(X) for a variety X, because this would be equivalent to a map i: Spec k →X, but pulling
back by this map is never faithful unless X itself is a point.
So having a ﬁber functor is sort of perpendicular to being a scheme. You can think of it as saying that
Spec C admits a faithfully ﬂat map from a point, which is intuitively saying Spec C is a quotient of a point.
Stackiness is pretty important here, so that this can be nontrivial.

In particular (and this is what’s classically called Tannakian reconstruction), if G is an aﬃne algebraic
group, meaning a group object in the category of aﬃne varieties (so, G is an aﬃne variety and multiplication
and inversion are maps of varieties, as for GLn(k)), then Spec(RepG) = BG = pt/G, and the ﬁber functor is
identiﬁed with the quotient pt →pt/G. We can recover G as the relations we imposed.
The way this is usually phrased is that, given C and a ﬁber functor f, one can construct an aﬃne algebraic
group structue on Aut(f), and C ≃RepG.
There are lots of weird groups out there that have no ﬁnite-dimensional algebraic representations, i.e.
no maps to GLn(k). We won’t be able to see those from this perspective, and in general Spec(C) is the
proalgebraic completion of G, which is some technical thing you have to do to make it more algebraic. In a
sense, this proalgebraic completion is the universal algebraic group you can build out of G.
Now let’s go out in nature and look for symmetric monoidal categories with ﬁber functors. (Our examples
will be biased towards algebraic geometry, because our test categories are categories of R-modules. If you
worked with modules over C∗-algebras, or something like that, your examples will feel more topological.) We
can build groups by building categories with ﬁber functors, i.e. vector spaces with some structure.

Pontrjagin duality says that when G is a compact group, G∨is discrete, and compact groups have semisimple
representation categories; in the algebraic case, semisimple representation categories mean reductive groups.
G is reductive iﬀQC(pt/G) = RepG is semisimple. You can carry over a fair amount of the story of Pontrjagin

Example 14.9. This example is the reason for the name “ﬁber functor.” Suppose X is a connected (and
locally path-connected) topological space, and consider the symmetric monoidal category Loc(X) of local
systems of vector spaces on X; the symmetric monoidal structure is pointwise tensor product.
Choose a basepoint x ∈X; taking the ﬁber at x deﬁnes a functor Loc(X) →Vect; because tensor product
is pointwise, this is a symmetric monoidal functor, and because X is connected, this functor is faithful. So
taking the ﬁber is a ﬁber functor.
We therefore obtain a group G such that Loc(X) = RepG. This is essentially π1(X, x), because repre-
sentations of π1 on k-vector spaces are idntiﬁed via monodromy with local systems of k-vector spaes on X.
However, any group can be a π1, but not all groups have ﬁnite-dimensional algebraic representations, hence
“essentially” above — in general, you will obtain that G is the proalgebraic completion of π1(X).
(

a faithful symmetric monoidal functor, identifying C with RepG for some group G, and in fact G = Gal(k/k).
This is a form of Galois descent: Galois groups come from automorphism groups of ﬁeld extensions, and we’re
seeing that here.
