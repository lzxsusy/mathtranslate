Last time, we discussed among other things cohomological automorphic forms, such as H∗(G(Z)\GR/K),
which is fortunate cases is isomorphc to the group cohomology of G(Z). The function-ﬁeld analogue is simpler:
functions on a discrete set BunG(C)(Fq).
One important question you might ask here is: this is supposed to be related to some 4d TFT AG. What
does this theory attach to a 3-manifold Y ? When G is a torus, we attached to Y the homology of the space
of G-connections on Y . But in general, it’s not clear what we should attach to a 3-manifold: this is an open
problem. This doesn’t doom the program to view geometric Langlands in terms of topological ﬁeld theory:
the conjectures themselves are about the categories attached to surfaces, and these we do know how to deﬁne.

As one example, suppose a ﬁnite group G acts on a set X and K ⊂G, and take C[X/K]. The coset space
X/K has an action of NG(K)/K, but this isn’t always very helpful: for some choices of G and K, this is
trivial. But functions on X/K has a better action.
We know that the algebra C[G] acts on the vector space C[X], and C[X/K] = C[X]K, the K-invariants. So
what acts on the K-invariants of a G-representation? Taking K-invariants is a functor (–)K : RepG →Vect,
and it is canonically representable:
(18.3)

(18.4)

(18.5)

So these are functions on C[G/K] = C[G]K. When G is inﬁnite we would get measures instead of functions.
Deﬁnition 18.6. The Hecke algebra HG,K is the algebra of endomorphisms of the K-invariants functor.
Since taking K-invariants is representable,
(18.7)
HG,K = EndRepG VG,K = HomC[G](VG,K, VG,K) = (VG,K)K = C[G/K]K = C[K\G/K].
For the most part, this is why functions on double cosets appear in math. A priori, functions on double
cosets don’t have an algebra structure, but this construction produces a canonical one.
A piece of abstract nonsense called the Barr-Beck theorem allows us to make the following argument: the
K-invariants functor RepG →Vect factors through RepG →ModHG,K: K-invariants canonically have this
extra structure. This forgetful functor forgets less than one might imagine: inside RepG, consider the full
subcategory whose K-invariants are “big enough,” meaning that they are generated as G-representations by
their K-invariants. For example, for irreducible representations, this is asking just that there is some nonzero
K-invariant vector. Just for fun, we will call these representations spherical.
The conclusion is that the forgetful functor from spherical representations to HG,K-modules is an equivalence
of categories! There’s nothing particularly representation-theoretic going on here; it’s all tautologies about
adjoint functors. Another way to say this fact is that the spherical category is generated by VG,K.
Much of this holds in greater generality, with the exception of a few times we use that functions and
measures on a ﬁnite set coincide. But the application of the Barr-Beck theorem carries forth.

Example 18.24. Pick a Lie group G and a Borel subgroup B and let K = B(Fq) inside G(Fq). For example,
if G = GLn, B can be chosen to be the subgroup of upper triangular matrices.
Now HG,K is the ﬁnite Hecke algebra, the algebra of B(Fq)-invariant functions on the ﬂag variety
G(Fq)/B(Fq). As a vector space, the Hecke algebra is C[W], where W is the Weyl group of G (e.g. Sn for
G = GLn), but the algebra structure is diﬀerent — it depends on the size q of the ﬁnite ﬁeld, and in a sense
is a q-deformation of C[W] with its usual algebra structure.
Write B = G(Fq)/B(Fq) and write the double coset spac as G(Fq)\B × B, giving us a relative version of
ﬂags. If G = SL2, so B = P1
Fq, then in a coordinate-independent way we can only ask whether two lines
are equal or not, so we should expect the B(Fq)-orbits on the pair of ﬂag varieties B × B to just have two
elements: the orbit where the two ﬂags are equal, and the orbit where they’re unequal. This expectation is
correct. Therefore the Hecke algebra is two-dimensional, spanned by {1, T}; on the equal orbit, T ∗T = q,
and on the unequal orbit, T ∗T = q −1. That is, you ask how many things are diﬀerent from the ﬂags you
had: if they’re equal, there’s q points, and if they’re not, there are only q −1. Notice tht q = #(A1
Fq), and
q −1 = #(GmFq). Collating this together,
(18.25)
T 2 = (q −1)T + q,
or equivalently (T + 1)(T −q) = 0. If you set q = 1, you get T 2 = 1, the group algebra of the Weyl group
Z/2. This is a little spooky (what’s the ﬁeld with one element?), but motivates the idea that in general, this
Hecke algebra is a q-deformation of C[Z/2]. As we’ve constructed it, the Hecke algebra is a C-algebra, but
the relations are ﬁnite Laurent series in q, so Hq is actually a Z[q, q−1]-algebra, so we can evaluate at q = 1.
So maybe G(F1) = W, though there’s no way to make that make sense precisely.

This demonstrates an important point: the Hecke algebra in this case is a q-deformation of the Weyl
group, and therefore is at least as noncommutative as the Weyl group. And the Weyl group can be plenty
noncommutative, e.g. for GLn we get C[Sn] for the symmetric group Sn. Hecke algebras are not commutative
in general.
But in some cases they are commutative. For an arbitrary group K, G := K ×K where K ,→G diagonally,
HG,K is commutative! The double coset space is
(18.26)
K\K × K/K = K/K,
where K acts on K by conjugation. When you take functions, the algebra structure you get is that of class
functions, i.e. functions on K/K with pointwise multiplication, and this is commutative. In fact, this is
(18.27)
HK×K,K = EndC[K]⊗C[K]op C[K] = Z(C[K]).
This is the center of the algebra, also known as the zeroth Hochschild homology of C[K]. The identiﬁcation
of the center with EndA⊗Aop(A) is not a special fact about group algebras; it holds for associative algebras in
general. So this Hecke algebra is not just commutative, it’s commutative in a very robust way.
We will see that the Hecke algebras that act on automorphic forms, which are large and interesting because
we began with a huge amount of symmetry, are commutative algebras! This is the birth of the Langlands
program. In fact, they are commutative for structural reasons, but this is less obvious. We will use topological
ﬁeld theory to demonstrate this.
