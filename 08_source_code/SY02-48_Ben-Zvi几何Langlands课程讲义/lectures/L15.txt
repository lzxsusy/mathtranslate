Lecture 15.
1-Shifted Cartier duality: 3/23/21
Since we just came back from spring break, let’s review what we were doing when we last met, which was
to interpret Tannakian reconstruction as a categoriﬁed analogue of the adjunction between taking functions
on a variety and taking Spec of a commutative ring. From this perspective, we recover RepG as the category
of quasicoherent sheaves on the stack pt/G, for G an aﬃne algebraic group.
We further interpreted this via a spectral decomposition (in the context of Fourier-Mukai duality, or
1-shifted Cartier duality): if G is an abelian group and µ: G × G →G is its multiplication map, then
pushforward by multiplication deﬁnes a convolution symmetric monoidal product on QC(G). For example,
take (QC)(Z) (or Z-graded vector spaces); its spectrum is BGm, because this category is equivalent, as
symmetric monoidal categories, to the category of Gm-representations. The “shift” is because we started
with Z and obtained BGm, rather than Gm.
More generally, suppose G is a ﬁnite abelian group scheme, and let G∨:= Spec(C[G], ∗). Functions on G
under convolution are therefore identiﬁed with functions on G∨under pointwise multiplication. And one level
up, RepG ≃QC(G∨). The shift appears again, because RepG ≃QC(BG): passing to the categorical level,
one of the sides has to be moved down.
Let’s step back and ask, what is G∨[1], the Fourier-Mukai dual? What are its points? We deﬁned it via
its functor of points; for a ﬁeld k, the k-points of G∨[1] are symmetric monoidal functors
(15.1)
(Shv(G), ∗) ≃(QC(G), ∗) −→Vectk.
You can think of these as “categorical one-dimensional representations of (QC(G), ∗)” — an uncategoriﬁed
representation is a map G →GL1(k) or, equivalently, an algebra homomorphism C[G] →End(k); here we
have a symmetric monoidal functor to End(Vectk) = Vectk.
Therefore G should map to Aut(Vectk), which is the symmetric monoidal category of lines (⊗-invertible
vector spaces, or equivalently one-dimensional vector spaces) with tensor product. This object, which is
equivalent to BGm, is a “group,” or more precisely a group object in stacks. That is, its functor of points is
valued not in abelian groups (like for a group scheme), nor just in groupoids (like for an ordinary stack), but
in Picard groupoids: symmetric monoidal groupoids in which all objects are ⊗-invertible.
Spelling this out a little more, the maps X →BGm are the symmetric monoidal category of line bundles
on X, with tensor product. These are all invertible under tensor product. Also, why BGm? The idea is that
all invertible k-vector spaces are isomorphic, but they have a Gm of isomorphisms, so this stack is equivalent
to pt/Gm, which was our original deﬁnition of BGm.
And part of the upshot is that the points of Spec(Shv(G), ∗) are categoriﬁed characters or character sheaves:
group (stack) homomorphisms G →BGm. These are equivalent to multiplicative line bundles L →G, i.e.
Lg ∗Lh ∼= Lgh or µ∗L = L ⊠L. So
(15.2)
G∨[1] ∼= HomGrp(G, BGm) = {multiplicative line bundles}.
And this is completely analogous to what we did with Cartier duality before: we deﬁned the dual using
Spec(C[G], ∗) (just functions, not categoriﬁed), and observed this is a group algebra.
Remark 15.3. This shifted duality is happening in the derived category of sheaves of abelian groups on the
category of aﬃne schemes — the usual derived category, but in a more universal sense. Group schemes and
group stacks deﬁne objects in this category. Because this category is triangulated, it makes sense to take a
shift here, and shifted Cartier duality and what we called G∨[1] is shifted in this triangulated sense.

Suppose C is a Riemann surface; then Jac(C) ≃Jac(C)∗, and Pic(C), the stack of line bundles on C, is
equivalent to Pic(C) × BGm (here Pic(C) is the usual, non-stacky thing we called Pic(C)). The non-stacky
Pic(C) splits as Z × Jac(C) × BGm; when you dualize, Z ↔BGm and Jac(C) is self-dual, so Pic(C) is also
self-dual, but in an interesting way: the degree and the Gm-stabilizer are exchanged.

Example 15.11 (de Rham class ﬁeld theory). We can also do this in the de Rham setting. This has a
diﬀerent ﬂavor: the two sides of the duality are more symmetric (yay!) but more complicated (darn).
Given a variety X, we will introduce the de Rham space XdR as the quotient of X by the equivalence
relation x ∼y if x and y are inﬁnitesimally close. Formally, this is the quotient of X by a formal neighborhood
of X in the diagonal. This is not a scheme, but its functor of points is well-deﬁned enough. Why introduce
this object? It is useful for seting up diﬀerential operators in algebraic geometry.
Quasicoherent sheaves on the formal neighborhood of the diagonal are quasicoherent sheaves on X with
data Fx ∼= Fy when x and y are inﬁnitesimally close. It turns out that QC(XdR) ≃ModDX, the category of
D-modules on X. On aﬃne opens U, these are data of a quasicoherent sheaf F together with an action of the
ring of diﬀerential operators D(U) on F(U).
Diﬀerential operators are generated by functions and vector ﬁelds, and we already have an OX-action on
F, so a D-module structure is equivalent to specifying how vector ﬁelds act. This can be speciﬁed as a map
TX →End(F) satisﬁng ﬂatness, that [ξ1, ξ2] acts by the commutator of (ξ1·) and (ξ2·), and a Leibniz rule
(15.12)

So these are the same thing as ﬂat connections on X.
