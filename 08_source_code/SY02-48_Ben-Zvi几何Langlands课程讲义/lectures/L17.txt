Lecture 17.
The moduli stack of principal G-bundles on a curve: 3/30/21
We want to generalize this abelian duality that we’ve seen in the previous lectures to the case where G
is a nonabelian group. There is an early, diﬃcult problem: C[G] is noncommutative, so there’s no obvious
dual Spec(C[G], ∗). So to sidestep this issue and obtain a duality, we need to ﬁnd something else which is
commutative. We will use topological ﬁeld theory to do this.
There are two places that you could start this story, from the problem or from the solution (TFT), and
we’ll start with the problem. We speciﬁcally want to replace what appeared in class ﬁeld theory, Pic(C),
with a nonabelian counterpart: the theory of automorphic forms.
Let C be a smooth projective curve over a ﬁeld k, which is either Fq or C (in the latter case, C is a
Riemann surface). In the abelian case, we studied functions on C (k = Fq) or sheaves (more general k, in
particular k = C) on Pic(C), the moduli space of line bundles on C. We will replace “line bundles” here with
vector bundles or, more generally, principal G-bundles.
Let G be an algebraic group over k, aﬃne and (usually) reductive. Rather than deﬁne reductive right now,
we’ll say that this includes the usual suspects such as GLn, SOn, and Spn. A principal G-bundle is a space P
with a map P →C and a right G-action on C such that this locally on C, P|U ∼= U × G, where G acts on G
but not on U. When k = Fq, we have to be speciﬁc about what “locally” means: we mean étale-locally. For
G = GLn, this is equivalent data to a rank-n vector bundle. Principal bundles on a space form a groupoid: if
you write down the notion of a map of principal bundles, analogously to the deﬁnition of a map of vector
bundles, you will ﬁnd all such maps are isomorphisms. In particular, principal bundles have automorphisms.
Let BunG(C) be the moduli space (stack) of principal G-bundles on C. The functor of points of BunG(C)
sends a ring R to the groupoid of principal G-bundles on C × Spec R. When G = GL1 = Gm, then there is

First, any principal G-bundle on C is generically trivial. Some thought is required to see why this is true;
it unpacks to ﬁnding a rational section of P →C, meaning a section on C minus ﬁnitely many points. And a

transition functions between these descriptions. We need as an important ingredient the Beauville-Laszlo
theorem, which says that we can actually glue a G-bundle from formal transition data.

Example 17.11. Say G = GL2, in which case this relates to something much more classical. We’re asking
approximately what is the moduli space of rank 2 vector bundles on Spec(Z), or some sort of completion of
Spec(Z)?
Very naïvely, this should be something like a rank 2 free Z-module, but with a trivialization at inﬁnity.
Let B be the space of R-bases of C, which can be identiﬁed with the space of isomorphisms Z2 ⊗R →R2;
ﬁxing a favorite basis identiﬁes B ∼= GL2(R).
We can describe B concretely as C× × C \ R by choosing the ﬁrst basis vector, then the second. If L is the
space of lattices in C, i.e. free rank-2 Z-modules Λ with an isomorphism Λ ⊗R ∼= C, then L is close to the
space B of bases, but we’re identifying bases under invertible integer-valued transformations:
(17.12)
L ∼= GL2(Z)\B.
Because we’re using C, this space has a natural complex structure. This does not generalize to higher rank.
There are several variants of this, e.g. L(N), lattice where we also ﬁx a basis mod N, i.e. an identiﬁcation
L/N ∼= (Z/N)⊕2. You can think of this as a rank-2 bundle and a trivialization on (N) ⊂Spec Z. This uses
multplicity, e.g. (p) and (p2) are diﬀerent, trivializing on higher-order neighborhoods of the point p ∈Spec Z.
Another variant is L, the space of lattices up to C-homothety,21 which is a rescaling operation. This space
is

An analogous approach works for other groups. For example, when G = On, one forms a moduli space of
orthogonal bundles: ﬁx an even lattice Λ and look at free Z-modules V equipped with a quadratic function
q: V →Z. Given this data, we can make sense of when an automorphism of V is orthogonal, i.e. when it
preserves q, so we get the orthogonal group O(V, q). Then we get the double quotient
(17.16)
Oq(Q)\Oq(A)/Oq(OA).
This has a very classical meaning: the (isomorphism classes of) quadratic forms in the genus of q, meaning
that they identify with q mod every N. Things like Θ-series are certain functions on these collections of
quadratic forms.
And we can play this game for any group G over a number ﬁeld F, deﬁning “BunG” as
(17.17)
G(F)\G(AF )/(G(OAF ) × K∞),
where K∞is some compact subgroup of G(F ⊗Q R) (F ⊗Q R is called the Archimedean completion of F, and
is some product of copies of R and C). This is the data at inﬁnity for general F. (17.17) is an arithmetic
locally symmetric space. G(F ⊗Q R) factors as G(R)×s × G(C)×t, and K∞has to have components in all of
these.
Sometimes one wants to consider cohomological automorphic forms, where we replace functions on
G(F)\G(AF )/(G(OAF ) × K∞) with cohomology. Since GR/K∞is contractibe, this amounts to the group
cohomology
(17.18)
H∗(G(Z); C).
More generally, of G(Z) acts on a complex vector space V , we can consider H∗(G(Z); V ), which is the
cohomology of our arithmetic locally symmetric space twisted by some local system. There is a nonobvious
fact called the Eichler-Shimura isomorphism (using Hodge theory on SL2(Z)\H) which means that classical
modular forms sit inside cohomological modular forms for G = SL2. Rather than worry about holomorphic
functions with growth conditions, we can take cohomology.
In the function ﬁeld case, functions on the discrete set BunG(C)(Fq) can be identiﬁed with H∗(BunG(C)(Fq)),
and this is one model for the theory of automorphic forms. In general, the “same” automorphic forms appear
in very diﬀerent places under quite diﬀerent kinds of functions, and this is a quirk of the general theory. This
will lead to a notion of “geometric automorphic forms,” analogous to “geometric class ﬁeld theory.” We take
sheaves: if k is a ﬁeld and C a curve over k as before, we will consider sheaves on BunG(C), “automorphic
sheaves.” This is analogous to what we did in the abelian case, where we sent Σ to sheaves on Pic(Σ). We
hope these are attached to surfaces in a topological ﬁeld theory.
Another potential source of motivation is to look at number ﬁelds, where arithmetic locally symmetric
spaces appear in classical number theory, so their geometric analogues are hopefully the right thing to study.
The basic problem we run into is that BunG(C) is not a group, and does not have a natural group action
we can use to decompose things into eigenthings. Instead, Hecke operators will act, and excitingly they form
a commutative algebra, so we can do spectral decomposition. We will get into this more next time. This is
where ideas from ﬁeld theory shine.
